# Chapter 251: Optic Nerve and Visual Cortex BCI — When the Display Is a Nerve, Not a Panel

> **Part**: Part XXXI — Future Directions
> **Audience**: Graphics application developers and AR/VR engineers (Ch27, Ch166, Ch250) curious what a post-panel display target would mean for the stack; general readers of the book's XR chapters. This chapter is explicitly speculative and forward-looking, unlike the rest of the book.
> **Status**: First draft — 2026-08-29

Every other chapter in this book ends at a panel: a `VkImage` handed to a compositor, a swapchain presented to an `xrEndFrame` call, a Wayland buffer attached to a surface, a `DRM_MODE_ATOMIC_COMMIT` that lights up a physical display. The entire stack this book documents — kernel modesetting, Mesa, Wayland, OpenXR, the Meta XR SDK suite in Ch250 — exists to get pixels onto glass or a projector, then let an eye do the rest.

This chapter looks at what happens if the eye is removed from that chain: technology that stimulates the optic nerve or visual cortex directly, so a "display" is a pattern of electrical or optogenetic activation delivered to neural tissue rather than a pattern of photons delivered to a retina. This is a real, funded, publicly discussed research direction — one company (Science Corporation, discussed in §12) has said in public interviews that it sees a path to this being "the ultimate AR/VR display technology" — but as of this chapter's research (2026), essentially none of it is built for that purpose. Every device and trial covered here targets vision *restoration* for people who are already blind or severely low-vision. Using the same class of technology as a *display* for a sighted person is a different, harder, and far less mature idea, and §12 treats that distinction as load-bearing rather than incidental.

**A note on how this chapter differs from the rest of the book.** The book's stated standard is that every non-trivial claim carries a citable primary source, and that "Note: needs verification" callouts mark anything that couldn't be confirmed. That standard is followed strictly here, but the underlying field is much thinner and more volatile than kernel or Mesa source: several of the devices below are pre-clinical, several companies referenced have gone bankrupt or paused programs mid-chapter-research, and timeline claims are, by nature, promises rather than facts. §13 separates attributed, dated statements from specific companies (fact: "company X said Y on date Z") from a single clearly labeled paragraph of the author's own synthesis (opinion, not sourced fact). Where research turned up a plausible claim with no primary source, or a claim that could not be independently verified, that is stated explicitly rather than smoothed over.

## 1. Three Places to Intercept the Signal

"Optic nerve BCI" is the popular shorthand for a family of approaches that intercept the visual pathway at three anatomically distinct points, and the distinction matters for everything that follows:

- **Retina** — an implant sits on or under the retina itself, stimulating the retinal ganglion cells (or, in the optogenetic case, making them light-sensitive) before the optic nerve even begins. This requires the patient's retina and optic nerve to still be structurally present, which rules it out for some causes of blindness (glaucoma with optic nerve damage, traumatic nerve transection) but not others (retinitis pigmentosa, age-related macular degeneration, where the nerve is typically intact).
- **Optic nerve** — an electrode interfaces with the optic nerve trunk itself, downstream of the retina, upstream of the brain. This is the anatomical target the chapter's title names, and — as §4 documents — it is also the least actively developed of the three today.
- **Visual cortex** — an electrode array sits on or in the brain's visual cortex, bypassing the eye, optic nerve, and optic chiasm entirely. This is the only approach that works for patients with no eyes or a severed optic nerve, and it is where most current well-funded development is concentrated (§5).

This chapter covers all three, because "true" optic-nerve interfacing turns out to be a small and largely historical slice of the field — the retina and the cortex are where the money and the publications are. Readers should treat "optic nerve BCI" in the chapter title as the popular framing for this technology category, not a claim that the optic nerve itself is where the action is.

## 2. A Primer: Visual Acuity Units, Human (and Animal) Visual Capacity, and the Size of the Interface Gap

Every acuity figure in this chapter — Argus II's "logMAR 2.6–2.9," PRIMA's "20/418" — assumes the reader already knows what those units mean and what they're being measured against. This section supplies that background before the comparison table in §3, and closes with a plain-language explanation of why the interface gap the rest of the chapter documents is as large as it is.

**Snellen notation** is the familiar "20/X" fraction from an eye chart. The numerator is the test distance (20 feet, or 6 meters in the metric "6/X" form); the denominator is the distance at which a person with statistically typical vision could just resolve the same letter. "20/20" does not mean perfect vision — it is a historical clinical convention for a common level of acuity, not a hard physiological ceiling; some individuals test better (20/15, 20/10). "20/200" means a person must stand 20 feet from a letter that someone with typical vision could read from 200 feet away — this is also the U.S. Social Security Administration's statutory threshold for legal blindness in the better eye, with correction. [Source: SSA, 20 CFR §404.1581](https://www.ssa.gov/OP_Home/cfr20/404/404-1581.htm) The Snellen fraction is a ratio, not an interval scale, which makes it awkward for research: the visual difference between 20/20 and 20/40 is not "the same size" as the difference between 20/200 and 20/220, so Snellen scores can't be meaningfully averaged across patients or study visits.

**logMAR** (log of the minimum angle of resolution) fixes that. The minimum angle of resolution (MAR) is the angular size, in minutes of arc, of the smallest detail a person can resolve; logMAR is simply log₁₀ of that value, which converts Snellen's ratio scale into an additive one that clinical researchers can average. [Source: Webvision/NCBI Bookshelf, *Visual Acuity*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK11509/) Normal vision (20/20) is logMAR 0.0; each +0.1 corresponds to losing one line on a standard ETDRS eye chart, and — critically for reading this chapter's figures — logMAR runs in the *opposite* direction from Snellen's numerator convenience: higher logMAR is worse vision, and each whole-number increase is a roughly tenfold jump in the size of detail needed to see anything at all.

| logMAR | Snellen (approx.) | What it corresponds to in this chapter |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | 20/20 | Normal reference vision |
| ~0.3–0.4 | 20/40–20/50 | Typical driving-license threshold in most U.S. states |
| 1.0 | 20/200 | U.S. legal blindness threshold |
| ~1.3 | ~20/400–20/420 | PRIMA's documented outcomes (§3) |
| 2.6–2.9 | ~20/12,000–20/16,000 | Argus II's documented outcomes (§3) |

**Human visual capacity**, for scale: a healthy human retina contains on average about 92 million rods and 4.6 million cones — direct anatomical counts from whole-mounted retinas, somewhat lower than the round "120 million / 6 million" figures still common in older textbooks. [Source: Curcio et al., *Human Photoreceptor Topography*, 1990, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2324310/?dopt=Abstract) Cone density peaks at the fovea, averaging about 199,000 cones/mm² (individual variation is large — 100,000 to 324,000/mm²), and it is this tight foveal packing, not any property of the optic nerve or brain, that sets the 20/20 reference point. Even that isn't the ceiling: because the visual system does additional neural processing on top of raw photoreceptor spacing, humans routinely show "hyperacuity" — detecting misalignments finer than a single photoreceptor's width — which is one reason 20/20 is a convention rather than a hard limit.

Acuity varies enormously across species — over four orders of magnitude among animals with image-forming eyes — and a few sourced data points bracket where the devices in this chapter sit relative to biology generally, not just human biology. [Source: Caves, Brandley & Johnsen, *Visual Acuity and the Evolution of Signals*, Trends in Ecology & Evolution, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29609907/) The wedge-tailed eagle has been behaviorally measured at roughly 132–143 cycles/degree of grating resolution, reported as a Snellen equivalent near 20/5 — about four times sharper than the human reference point. [Source: PMC, *High resolution of colour vision, but low contrast sensitivity in a diurnal raptor*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6125913/) The domestic cat, by contrast, behaviorally resolves only about 6 cycles/degree — despite retinal and optical hardware capable of roughly 20–30 cycles/degree — which works out to a Snellen equivalent near 20/100 to 20/150; cats trade acuity for rod-dominated low-light sensitivity. [Source: Blake, Cool & Crawford, *Visual resolution in the cat*, Vision Research, 1974, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0042698974902181) The laboratory mouse, measured behaviorally with the visual water task, resolves only about 0.49 cycles/degree. [Source: Prusky, West & Douglas, *Behavioral assessment of visual acuity in mice and rats*, Vision Research, 2000, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S004269890000081X) Converting that last figure to a Snellen-style equivalent using the same cycles/degree-to-acuity relationship applied above (roughly 30 cycles/degree ≈ 20/20) gives approximately 20/1200 — a derived approximation rather than a directly reported Snellen figure, since the original study measured cycles/degree, not letter or grating acuity in Snellen terms; it is included here as a rough anchor, not a precise clinical equivalent.

**The interface gap, in plain terms.** Natural human vision starts with roughly 97 million photoreceptors per eye, which the retina's own circuitry compresses down to about 1.2 million optic-nerve fibers (§8) before the signal even leaves the eye — itself a roughly 80-to-1 compression the eye performs invisibly. Every device in this chapter tries to recreate a "display" using a channel count in the tens to low thousands: Argus II's 60 electrodes, PRIMA's 378 pixels, ICVP's 544 electrodes at the high end (§3, §5). That is four to five orders of magnitude below the eye's own photoreceptor count, and roughly three orders of magnitude below what the optic nerve itself carries after the eye's own compression. Put concretely: a single PRIMA pixel, at 100-micron pitch, does the job that many thousands of foveal cones would normally share between them. This is also why *where* the interface sits changes the nature of the problem rather than just its severity (§1): a retinal implant inherits the retina's still-intact, spatially coherent map and simply has too few electrodes for it (§7); an optic-nerve interface additionally loses the map itself, because the nerve's fascicles scramble spatial order during compression (§8, §9) — which is the specific, anatomical reason the optic-nerve interception point in this chapter's own title is the least developed of the three (§4); and a cortical interface has, in principle, the most neural real estate to work with, but is limited by surgical risk and array engineering rather than nerve anatomy, which is why cortical channel counts are the highest being attempted despite that target being the most invasive to reach (§1, §5).

## 3. State of the Art: What Already Works

A small number of retinal implants have reached human patients and regulatory approval, though "works" here means measurable, clinically documented benefit far short of normal vision — not anything resembling a video feed.

**Argus II (Second Sight)** was a 60-electrode epiretinal array (a 6×10 grid of 200µm platinum discs) that received FDA approval under a Humanitarian Device Exemption in 2013. [Source: Frontiers in Neuroscience, 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2018.00584/full) Long-term follow-up reported grating-acuity outcomes in the range of logMAR 2.6–2.9 — roughly 20/12,000 to 20/16,000 Snellen-equivalent, far below anything usable for reading or face recognition, though sufficient for some patients to detect motion, locate light sources, and navigate. [Source: Retina Today, 2014](https://retinatoday.com/articles/2014-oct/long-term-results-for-a-retinal-prosthesis-show-positive-outcomes) Second Sight discontinued manufacturing and implantation in May 2019 for financial reasons, leaving over 350 patients worldwide with obsolete, unsupported hardware still implanted in their eyes. [Source: Fierce Biotech](https://www.fiercebiotech.com/medtech/second-sights-bionic-eyes-have-gone-obsolete-while-still-implanted-report-finds) Second Sight itself pivoted to the cortical approach covered in §5 as Cortigent.

**PRIMA (Pixium Vision, later Science Corporation)** is a subretinal photovoltaic implant: 378 pixels at 100µm pitch, powered wirelessly by near-infrared light projected from a pair of glasses rather than a battery or inductive coil. [Source: PRIMA product site](https://prima.science.xyz/) Its theoretical acuity ceiling from pixel geometry alone is 20/420, and clinical results have landed almost exactly there — 20/418 to 20/438 in reported cases. [Source: bioRxiv, pixel-pitch acuity modeling](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.06.29.498181.full.pdf) A 2025 *New England Journal of Medicine* report on the PRIMAvera trial (geographic-atrophy patients, the advanced form of dry AMD) found a mean improvement of 0.51 logMAR (25.5 letters) and a maximum of 1.18 logMAR (59 letters), with 84.4% of a roughly 38–47-patient cohort regaining some reading ability. [Source: Ophthalmology Science, 2024/2025](https://www.ophthalmologyscience.org/article/S2666-9145(24)00046-0/fulltext); [Source: Clinical Trials Arena](https://www.clinicaltrialsarena.com/news/bci-implant-trial-success-prefigures-new-era-in-eye-care/) Pixium Vision itself entered receivership in November 2023 and judicial liquidation in February 2024. [Source: IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/retina-implant-pixium-sa-receivership) Its assets, including PRIMA and its ongoing trials, were acquired in April 2024 by Science Corporation — a company founded by a former Neuralink president, discussed further in §6 and §12 for its separate optogenetic program. [Source: BusinessWire, April 2024](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20240425683676/en/Science-Corporation-Acquires-Pixium%E2%80%99s-PRIMA-Retinal-Implant-Extending-and-Diversifying-Its-Commitment-to-Vision-Restoration-Through-Brain-Computer-Interface-Technology) As of the most recent reporting found, PRIMA has received EU regulatory approval and Science Corp has raised a $230M Series C round while awaiting an FDA decision. [Source: TechCrunch, July 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/22/science-corporations-vision-restoring-chip-wins-eu-approval/); [Source: TechCrunch, March 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/05/science-corp-closes-230m-round-as-it-pushes-to-get-its-brain-implant-to-patients/)

Two other retinal programs illustrate how commercially fragile this space has been. **Retina Implant AG's Alpha AMS** was discontinued in March 2019 after 16 years of development; the company dissolved, citing the EU regulatory and reimbursement climate and the absence of "groundbreaking" further progress, with only academic follow-up continuing at the University of Tübingen. [Source: BioRegio STERN](https://www.bioregio-stern.de/en/news/retina-implant-ag-discontinues-business-activities) **Bionic Vision Technologies** (Australia) reported a small (4-patient) Phase I/II trial of a second-generation suprachoroidal implant with 97% electrode functionality at 2.7 years and "substantial improvements in functional vision," but as of the source found is still pre-pivotal, with a larger multi-center trial toward FDA approval pending funding. [Source: Centre for Eye Research Australia](https://www.cera.org.au/bionic-eye-trial-reveals-substantial-vision-improvements/)

## 4. The Optic Nerve Specifically: Tried, and Largely Abandoned

The chapter's title names the optic nerve because that is the popular framing, but the research for this chapter turned up no confirmed, active, modern (2024–2026) human clinical program whose electrode interface is the optic nerve itself, as distinct from the retina or cortex. The one substantial historical effort illustrates why.

**Claude Veraart's MiViP project** (Belgium) implanted a spiral cuff electrode wrapped circumferentially around the optic nerve — a non-penetrating design borrowed from peripheral-nerve stimulation. The first patient (1998) had a 4-contact cuff, later upgraded to an implanted stimulator in 2000; a second patient (2004) received an 8-contact intraorbital spiral electrode. [Source: Springer, *Optic Nerve Visual Prosthesis*](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-0-387-77261-5_2); [Source: bionic-vision.org](https://www.bionic-vision.org/implants/mivip) The device could elicit phosphenes in different visual-field quadrants and produce "simple geometrical patterns" under open-loop stimulation, but the directly documented outcome was that stimulation produced "large, diffuse phosphenes" rather than a spatially localized, closed-loop-controllable pattern. [Source: PubMed, *Visual sensations produced by optic nerve stimulation using an implanted self-sizing spiral cuff electrode*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9824694/) No acuity or reading-level outcome was ever reported, and the project did not progress beyond these two patients. This chapter's research did not find an explicit retrospective document from the project team stating why it was discontinued — that specific causal claim is inferred from the technical result (diffuse, unlocalizable phosphenes), not sourced to a project statement, and should be read as such.

The underlying reason a cuff electrode struggles here is anatomical, and §8 covers it in more depth: the optic nerve does not preserve the retina's tidy 2D spatial map. A circumferential electrode around the outside of the nerve activates whatever fascicles happen to be nearest the contact, and those fascicles do not correspond to a clean, contiguous patch of visual field the way a matching patch of retina or cortex would.

The field's evident response to that failure was not to give up on the optic nerve, but to change *how* it is contacted. **OpticSELINE**, a project associated with Silvestro Micera's group (EPFL/Switzerland), adapts an intraneural (penetrating) 3D self-opening electrode array — originally designed for peripheral-nerve interfacing — to the optic nerve, with 12 stimulating contacts. Animal studies report more spatially selective visual-cortex activation than a surface cuff electrode achieves. [Source: Nature Biomedical Engineering, 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41551-019-0446-8); [Source: bioRxiv preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/biorxiv/early/2018/05/07/311035.full.pdf) As of this chapter's research, OpticSELINE remains preclinical (animal-only) — there is no human trial to report.

One further item surfaced in research deserves a clear disambiguation rather than inclusion in the vision-restoration narrative above: a currently enrolling trial (NCT06693882, "Eyetronic Nextwave System") does involve electrical stimulation of the optic nerve, but for glaucoma neuroprotection — slowing or reversing visual-field loss — using transcutaneous (external, non-implanted) stimulation. [Source: ctv.veeva.com trial registry](https://ctv.veeva.com/study/electrical-stimulation-of-the-optic-nerve-in-patients-with-glaucoma) It is not a prosthetic-vision or phosphene-generation program and should not be read as evidence of an active optic-nerve visual-prosthesis trial.

## 5. The Cortical Frontier: What's Nearly Possible

Bypassing the eye and optic nerve entirely by interfacing directly with the visual cortex is where most current well-funded activity sits, precisely because it works even when the eye and optic nerve are destroyed or absent.

**Orion**, developed by Second Sight and now continued by Cortigent (a Vivani Medical subsidiary), is a 60-electrode array placed on the surface of the visual cortex, paired with camera glasses and a wirelessly powered implantable pulse generator. [Source: Cortigent](https://www.cortigent.com/orion) Its Early Feasibility Study enrolled 6 patients in 2018–2019 and concluded in March 2025 with six years of follow-up data: all 6 patients showed improved performance on visual-function tasks device-on versus off, electrode failure was under 4%, and there was one serious adverse event (a seizure), managed by adjusting stimulation parameters, with no further serious events reported. [Source: Vivani/Cortigent press release](https://investors.vivani.com/investors/news-events/press-releases/detail/212/vivani-subsidiary-cortigent-presents-promising-6-year-early) Orion holds FDA Breakthrough Device Designation; as of the sourced material it has not yet entered a pivotal trial or received marketing authorization.

**ICVP**, the Intracortical Visual Prosthesis project (Illinois Institute of Technology, with the Chicago Lighthouse and Rush University), takes a structurally different approach: rather than one large array, it uses a Wireless Floating Microelectrode Array design — many small, independently wireless-powered 5mm modules, each carrying 16 electrodes, with no percutaneous connector. [Source: IIT News](https://www.iit.edu/news/third-successful-implantation-wireless-visual-prosthesis-brain-implant-continues-redefine) The first patient (February 2022) received 25 modules (400 electrodes); over two years of testing showed improved navigation and basic visually-guided task performance. A third patient, per IIT's most recent reporting, received 34 modules (544 electrodes) — the largest intracortical array reported for this application. [Source: IIT News](https://www.iit.edu/news/third-successful-implantation-wireless-visual-prosthesis-brain-implant-advances-frontier-artificial) The distributed multi-module wireless architecture is worth noting as a distinct engineering approach from Orion's single large array — it sidesteps some of the single-point-of-failure and connector-reliability issues that afflict monolithic arrays (§9).

**Gennaris** (Monash Vision Group, Australia) uses 9mm×9mm cortical tiles carrying penetrating microelectrode arrays, wirelessly linked to headgear-mounted camera and vision-processing hardware. The first-generation design specifies 473 electrodes, with a stated future direction toward 1,000+ channels via smaller, more numerous tiles. [Source: Monash Vision Group](https://www.monash.edu/bioniceye/technology) As of the most recent source found (late 2025), the project is still seeking funding for a first-in-human trial — no clinical data exists yet, and this chapter's research could not confirm whether a trial has since begun; treat Gennaris's clinical status as unconfirmed and possibly stalled.

Two newer entrants illustrate that this remains an active area of investment rather than a settled field. **ReVision Implant** (Belgium), with its "Occular" visual-cortex system, received FDA Breakthrough Device Designation in March 2026 and an oversubscribed €4M funding round; the company has stated plans for an acute intraoperative safety/feasibility trial in epilepsy-surgery patients in October 2026, with first human blind-patient trials expected in 2027. [Source: Med-Tech Insights](https://med-techinsights.com/2026/03/09/fda-grants-breakthrough-device-designation-to-revision-implant-visual-cortex-prosthesis/); [Source: Medical Device Network](https://www.medicaldevice-network.com/news/revision-implant-eyes-fih-trial-for-bci-following-fda-breakthrough-designation/); [Source: Tech Funding News](https://techfundingnews.com/the-bionic-eye-revision-implant-lands-e4m-for-a-vision-device-that-feeds-directly-into-your-brain/) **Neuralink's "Blindsight"** received FDA Breakthrough Device Designation in September 2024. [Source: nmn.com](https://www.nmn.com/news/could-the-blind-see-again-neuralinks-blindsight-clears-fda-hurdle) Its rationale — working even for patients with no eyes or a destroyed optic nerve — is the same cortical-bypass logic as Orion and ICVP. Company statements describe multi-year non-human-primate testing, but this chapter's research found no independent, peer-reviewed publication of that data; treat NHP-testing claims as company statements, not independently confirmed science. A first-human-implant target of "late 2025" was later reported to have slipped toward 2026; this is a moving, company-stated target rather than a fact, and is treated that way in §13. [Source: Newsweek](https://www.newsweek.com/elon-musk-neuralink-blindsight-brain-chip-2052917); [Source: Digital Health News](https://www.digitalhealthnews.com/elon-musk-s-neuralink-to-implant-blindsight-chip-in-first-human-by-2025)

## 6. Optogenetics: A Different Mechanism Entirely

Every device in §3 and §5 works by delivering electrical current directly to neurons through a physical electrode. Optogenetics is mechanistically distinct: a gene-therapy vector (typically an AAV2 viral vector) is used to make retinal ganglion cells — which are not naturally light-sensitive — express a light-sensitive protein, so they respond directly to light. A wearable device (typically stimulating goggles) then amplifies and re-encodes the visual scene into a light pattern those cells can respond to. No electrode implant is required at all, though a device is still needed to deliver the encoded light.

**GenSight Biologics' PIONEER trial** (GS030) is the most advanced human program using this mechanism, targeting retinitis pigmentosa. The first subject was enrolled on 2018-10-26. [Source: GenSight press release](https://www.gensight-biologics.com/2018/10/26/gensight-biologics-enrolls-first-subject-in-first-in-man-pioneer-phase-i-ii-clinical-trial-of-gs030-combining-gene-therapy-and-optogenetics-for-the-treatment-of-retinitis-pigmentosa/) A May 2021 case report in *Nature Medicine* documented a legally blind, Usher-syndrome patient regaining the ability to locate, count, and reach for objects, with EEG confirming visual-cortex activation during the tasks. [Source: GenSight press release on the Nature Medicine report](https://www.gensight-biologics.com/2021/05/25/gensight-biologics-announces-nature-medicine-case-report-showing-visual-recovery-after-gs030-optogenetic-treatment/) The FDA granted Fast Track Designation in October 2021. [Source: BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20211011005660/en/GenSight-Biologics-Announces-FDA-Grant-of-Fast-Track-Designation-for-Optogenetic-Therapy-GS030-as-Treatment-for-Retinitis-Pigmentosa) As of the most recent source found (May 2026), the trial remains Phase I/II, and the company reported that it had "decided to reassess the timeline and contents of its application" to the UK's MHRA — i.e., its regulatory submission is delayed and under reassessment, with no confirmed new date. [Source: BioSpace, May 2026](https://www.biospace.com/press-releases/gensight-biologics-announces-the-results-of-its-combined-general-meeting-of-may-19-2026)

**Science Corporation's "Science Eye"** combines the same optogenetic gene-therapy mechanism with an implanted flexible thin-film microLED display panel placed over the retina — a hybrid of optogenetics and a literal micro-display, rather than external goggles — targeting retinitis pigmentosa and dry AMD, with a stated resolution target of roughly eight times the pixel density of a contemporary phone display. [Source: Science Corp, "Announcing the Science Eye," Nov 2022](https://science.xyz/news/announcing-the-science-eye/) The company's 2022 announcement stated its near-term focus was "demonstrating sufficient safety data in animals so that we can begin a first-in-human clinical study" — i.e., preclinical at that time. ## 7. Obstacle 1: Electrode Density vs. Required Resolution

Every system in the comparison table falls far short of normal acuity, and the gap is not a matter of degree — it is close to two orders of magnitude in electrode/pixel count from what published modeling suggests is needed for basic functional tasks.

A widely cited simulation-based estimate (Cha, Horch & Normann, 1992) found that roughly 625 electrodes arranged in a 1cm×1cm cortical patch near the foveal representation could support approximately 20/30 acuity — near-normal. [Source: Springer, *Simulation of a phosphene-based visual field*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02368135); [Source: PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1510295/) More recent work using simulated prosthetic vision (SPV — showing sighted subjects a phosphene-degraded video feed to test what resolution supports what task) puts the object-recognition threshold at roughly 256–576 phosphenes, while current human visual-cortex prostheses elicit on the order of 88 phosphenes from a 10×10 electrode array. [Source: bioRxiv, "Minimizing phosphenes for object recognition," 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.26.614440v2.full) Face recognition appears to need somewhat fewer phosphenes than general object recognition — accuracy comparable to normal vision has been demonstrated with roughly a 25×21 grid, and caricaturing techniques can push usable identity recognition down to a 32×32 grid with 30% electrode dropout. [Source: ScienceDirect, *Face identity recognition in simulated prosthetic vision*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0042698917301116) Phosphene size matters independently of count: in one detection-task study, 100µm phosphenes significantly outperformed 300µm and 500µm phosphenes (d′ of 0.81 vs. 0.6 vs. 0.52). [Source: PMC, *Real-world indoor mobility with SPV*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8819280/)

The practical upshot: ICVP's 544-electrode array (§5) is the largest deployed to date and sits within the *lower* end of the object-recognition-threshold range from SPV modeling, while Orion's 60 electrodes and the historical Veraart cuff's 4–8 contacts are one to two orders of magnitude below any of these thresholds. Reaching even "basic functional vision" by these estimates requires roughly an order of magnitude more channels than any device deployed in a human as of this chapter's research.

## 8. Obstacle 2: Nerve Bandwidth and the Fascicular Problem

The human optic nerve carries approximately 1.0–1.5 million retinal ganglion cell axons (commonly cited as ~1.2 million) packed into a tract roughly 2mm in diameter. [Source: NCBI Bookshelf, *Neuroanatomy, Cranial Nerve 2*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK507907/); [Source: ScienceDirect, *Optic Nerve Fiber* overview](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/immunology-and-microbiology/optic-nerve-fiber) That number alone explains why the nerve is a harder interception point than either endpoint it connects: it is the visual pathway's bottleneck in terms of density, but not in terms of spatial organization.

The retina and visual cortex each preserve a coherent 2D retinotopic map — adjacent points in the visual field map to physically adjacent tissue, which is exactly the property a 2D electrode array (against the retina or the cortical surface) needs to exploit. The optic nerve does not preserve this cleanly. Retinal ganglion cell axons from different, sometimes distant, regions of the retina become interleaved into fascicles as they converge toward the optic disc; one documented example is that RGCs from just above and just below the retina's horizontal raphe send axons to diametrically opposite poles of the optic disc. Whatever apparent spatial order exists at the nerve is described in the literature as "a passive consequence of combining fascicles... not instructed to establish, nor constrained to maintain, a retinotopic order." [Source: PMC, *An update on visual prosthesis*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10668475/) This is the direct anatomical reason Veraart's cuff electrode (§4) produced large, diffuse phosphenes rather than a legible spatial pattern: a circumferential electrode contacts a fascicle bundle whose visual-field correspondence is scrambled relative to its physical position, not a clean patch of visual field the way a matching patch of retina or cortex would be. The field's response — moving from a circumferential cuff to a penetrating intraneural array (OpticSELINE, §4) that can target individual fascicles more precisely — is a direct engineering answer to this specific problem, though still unproven in humans.

## 9. What We Don't Know: No Human Fiber Map, and No Algorithm to Unscramble One

§8 establishes that the optic nerve scrambles the retina's 2D map rather than preserving it. A natural next question — especially given the "compression" framing used elsewhere in this chapter — is whether that scrambling is a recoverable code: something an algorithm could invert, the way a receiver reconstructs an interleaved signal once it knows the interleaving pattern. As of this chapter's research, no such algorithm exists, and the two reasons compound rather than being independent: the wiring diagram it would need to invert has never been measured in a living human at the required resolution, and the biology gives no reason to expect the mapping is even individually stable enough for one algorithm to work twice.

**It isn't decompression in the engineering sense.** The retina-to-optic-nerve pathway isn't a designed, reversible code with parity bits or known structure; it is the product of axon guidance during development, historically modeled by Sperry's chemoaffinity hypothesis — molecular gradients that steer each axon roughly, not a wiring plan anyone could read back out. Recovering spatial order from a jumbled fiber population is therefore an underdetermined inverse problem, closer to blind registration than to running a decompressor in reverse.

**What anatomy is actually known.** There is a coarse, circumferential retinotopic order in the orbital segment of the human optic nerve — fibers enter the nerve head along the margin of their retinal quadrant of origin, as a radial wedge, and pial septa keep bundles from that quadrant loosely separated for some distance. [Source: PMC, *Nerve fibre organisation in the human optic nerve and chiasm: what do we really know?*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11306597/) That order degrades intracranially: the septa disappear, fibers demonstrably move laterally between fascicles, and by the chiasm a 2024 review concludes the textbook assumption of preserved retinotopy "is probably not correct" — recent photomicroscopy even overturns the classic X-shaped chiasm diagram in favor of an H-shape, with paracentral rather than midline crossings. [Source: PMC, same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11306597/)

**What anatomy is not known, and why.** No 3D, fiber-resolved wiring diagram of a human optic nerve or chiasm exists. Diffusion tensor imaging, the usual non-invasive tool for tracing white-matter pathways, can't resolve individual axons — its voxels are far too coarse. There are no anterograde or retrograde tracing studies in humans of the kind that established retinotopic organization in animal models (goldfish, ferret, non-human primate); human evidence rests on a handful of degeneration and post-mortem photomicroscopy studies, including just one full photomicroscopic reconstruction of an entire human chiasm. [Source: PMC, same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11306597/) Fiber counts vary roughly three-fold between individuals (500,000 to 1,500,000 per nerve, against the ~1.2 million average cited elsewhere in this chapter, §8), which alone rules out a universal map even if the average case were fully characterized. Whether nearby fibers in a given electrode's contact zone stay retinotopically adjacent, whether fiber paths vary between individuals, and how much crossed and uncrossed fibers intermingle at the chiasm are all stated as open questions by the most recent review found on the topic, not settled facts with differing values. No imaging technique exists today that could build a usable per-patient fiber map before implantation.

**What's done instead: calibration, not decoding.** Every optic-nerve device covered in §4 substitutes measurement for computation. Veraart's spiral-cuff work established the pattern still used today — stimulate one electrode, ask the patient what and where they perceive, and build a lookup table from the responses. [Source: Springer, *Phosphene Mapping Techniques for Visual Prostheses*](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4419-0754-7_19) This is exhaustive rather than algorithmic — it doesn't scale past the tens-of-electrodes regime any of these devices have reached — depends on a patient who can reliably verbalize percept location, and degrades over the device's lifetime as the biocompatibility issues in §10 change the electrode-tissue interface. A related but distinct phenomenon is well documented at the *retinal* end of the pathway, before fibers even reach the nerve trunk: electrodes near the retina's nerve fiber layer activate passing axon bundles rather than just the cells directly beneath them, producing elongated "streak" or "wedge"-shaped phosphenes rather than clean points. A computational model fit to 55 human eyes' traced fiber-bundle trajectories from fundus photographs successfully predicts this distortion for retinal implants. [Source: Nature/Scientific Reports, Beyeler et al., *A model of ganglion axon pathways accounts for percepts elicited by retinal implants*, 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-45416-4) That model works precisely because the retinal nerve fiber layer's bundle trajectories — unlike the nerve trunk's internal fascicular structure — have actually been imaged and traced in living eyes, underscoring by contrast how much less is known about the nerve itself.

**Cortex is the partial exception, not a solved case.** Visual cortex retinotopy is comparatively well characterized — a stable log-polar (cortical-magnification) structure, now mappable per-subject from MRI using Human Connectome Project retinotopy atlases, letting some cortical-prosthesis research groups plan electrode placement computationally before surgery rather than purely by post-implant trial and error. [Source: PubMed, *A novel simulation paradigm utilising MRI-derived phosphene maps for cortical prosthetic vision*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37531948/); [Source: IOPscience, *Optimal placement of high-channel visual prostheses in human retinotopic visual cortex*](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1741-2552/adaeef) But this substitutes a better *prior* for a solved inverse problem, not the reverse: individual cortical folding (the calcarine sulcus's geometry varies person to person) and the same kind of axon-bundle-activation effect described above, at a cortical scale, mean per-subject perceptual calibration is still required after implantation — the map informs where to put electrodes, not what each one will actually produce.

**The open algorithmic question remains genuinely open.** Recent machine-learning work in this space — human-in-the-loop optimization of deep stimulus encoders — improves *what stimulation pattern to send* given an assumed or learned percept model, refined by iterative patient feedback. [Source: PMC, *Human-in-the-Loop Optimization for Deep Stimulus Encoding in Visual Prostheses*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11232484/) That is a different problem from inferring an unknown physical wiring diagram from population response statistics alone, without a supervised per-electrode calibration loop — this chapter's research found no published method that does the latter for optic-nerve or cortical interfaces. Whether one is even possible depends on facts about local fiber-order stability that, per the anatomy discussed above, nobody has yet measured.

## 10. Obstacle 3: Biocompatibility and Long-Term Stability

Any electrode implanted in neural tissue triggers a foreign body response: acute microglia and astrocyte activation begins at insertion and typically lasts 6–8 weeks, followed by chronic glial encapsulation of the electrode. [Source: PMC, *Long-Term Stability of Intracortical Microstimulation and Foreign Body Response Are Layer Dependent*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9234554/) This encapsulation raises electrode impedance over time — one study found impedance rising sharply from about one week post-implant and only stabilizing after roughly 18 weeks, with the exact time course varying by device. [Source: Frontiers in Neuroscience, *Electrochemical characterization and chronic stability of Utah electrode arrays*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2026.1789112/full) Rising impedance directly degrades the quality and reliability of both stimulation and recording over the life of an implant.

Mechanical failure compounds the biological response: in non-human primate studies, meningeal fibrosis and electrode-array ejection together account for roughly half of all chronic microelectrode-array failures — the single largest failure category, ahead of any electronic or connector fault. [Source: IOPscience, *Finite element model predicts micromotion-induced strain profiles*](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1741-2552/ae1bda) A five-year human retrospective study of intracortical microelectrode array performance exists and quantifies this degradation over a multi-year horizon, though it is not visual-prosthesis-specific. [Source: IOPscience, *Long-term intracortical microelectrode array performance in a human — 5 year retrospective*](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1741-2552/ac1add) Cortigent's Orion trial (§5) reported under 4% electrode failure over six years, which — while a promising result for that specific device — is the exception this section's general failure-rate literature makes clear is not guaranteed by the underlying biology.

## 11. Obstacle 4: Power, Telemetry, and Image-to-Stimulation Translation

Implanted stimulators need power and a data link without a percutaneous wire, both to avoid infection risk and because a wired connector is itself a common failure point. Current approaches split between inductive coupling (the long-standing approach, shared with cochlear implants), RF telemetry in the MedRadio (401–406MHz) or ISM (2.4GHz / 902–928MHz) bands, and emerging hybrid schemes combining galvanic intrabody communication with inductive power delivery. [Source: ResearchGate, systematic review of wireless power/data transmission for implanted devices](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/348359952_Wireless_Power_and_Data_Transmission_for_Implanted_Devices_via_Inductive_Links_A_Systematic_Review); [Source: arXiv, wireless power transmission for implantable medical devices](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.01240) This chapter's research did not find a specific, citable figure quantifying the tradeoff between channel count and power budget for any of the systems in §3/§5 — that specific number is a gap in the available literature, not a claim this chapter can make.

Separately from power, there is a purely algorithmic problem: converting a camera image into a stimulation pattern that a small number of electrodes can usefully convey is an open research problem, not a solved one. Naive downsampling of a camera feed to match electrode count performs poorly; recent work shows that semantic segmentation of the scene — identifying and simplifying to object boundaries rather than raw pixel intensity — outperforms saliency- or depth-based simplification for object-presence detection (F1 0.68 vs. 0.46 in one study). [Source: JOV/ARVO, "Enhancing SPV with deep learning-based scene simplification"](https://jov.arvojournals.org/article.aspx?articleid=2777381); [Source: arXiv, same work](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.00297) A further refinement uses a self-attention vision transformer (DINOv2 ViT) to emulate saccadic fixation — encoding only the most salient patches of a scene rather than the whole frame — and reaches roughly 91% classification accuracy on a simulated 14×14 phosphene grid, close to a healthy-control baseline of 92.76% in the same study. This is a concrete, citable near-state-of-the-art result, and it is worth noting for readers of this book specifically: it is a GPU deep-learning inference workload, the same class covered generally in Ch229, applied here to compress a visual scene down to what a handful of neural-interface channels can carry.

## 12. The Sighted-User Problem

Every device, trial, and roadmap in this chapter targets a person who is already blind or severely low-vision. None of the sourced material in §3–§7 describes implanting any of this technology into a sighted person to give them an *additional* or *replacement* display — that is a categorically different proposal, and the ethics and regulatory literature on invasive brain-computer interfaces reflects the difference.

Surveys of neurosurgical teams working on BCI report over 80% acceptability for rehabilitative use — restoring a lost or absent function — but explicitly lower and more divided acceptability for augmentation in healthy people, with military applications cited as the paradigm example of a less-acceptable use case. [Source: PMC, international survey of neurosurgical team acceptability of BCIs](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10444691/); [Source: arXiv, "Regulating Next-Generation Implantable BCIs"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.12540) This chapter's research did not find any source discussing "optic nerve or visual cortex BCI for AR/VR in a sighted person" specifically — the ethics literature addresses invasive BCI and neurotechnology generally, not this exact proposal. That is worth stating plainly: the idea of eye-bypassing AR/VR displays for sighted users is, as of this chapter's research, ahead of any ethics literature that names it directly. It should be read as extrapolation from general invasive-BCI ethics discussion, not as a directly sourced conclusion.

Against that backdrop, one on-record industry statement is directly relevant to this book's framing and worth quoting exactly rather than paraphrasing, because it is the only primary source found connecting this technology explicitly to AR/VR as a stated company vision rather than this chapter's own speculation:

> "We think we see a way to create what ends up as the ultimate AR/VR display technology without meaningful surgery." — Science Corporation, quoted in *Futurism*, January 2023. [Source: Futurism](https://futurism.com/the-byte/max-hodak-ultimate-vr-tech)

Two qualifications on that quote matter. First, the reporting frames it as referring to a *future variant* of Science Corp's technology using a different underlying mechanism than the microLED-over-retina device being developed for blindness restoration — it is a stated long-term aspiration, not a description of a shipping or funded product roadmap. Second, this chapter's research found no other company or research group making an equivalent public statement; it should be read as a single attributed claim from one company's founder, not as evidence of an industry-wide consensus that visual BCI is heading toward AR/VR use in sighted people.

## 14. What Changes in the Stack If the Display Is a Nerve

Every other chapter in this book ends the same way: a compositor commits a buffer, a scanout engine reads it, and a physical display converts it to light for an eye to receive. If the terminal endpoint becomes neural tissue rather than glass, several assumptions this book has made throughout stop holding, worth naming explicitly even though — per §12 and §13 — none of it is built or scheduled today:

- **The compositor's job changes fundamentally.** A Wayland or OpenXR compositor today reasons about pixels, refresh rate, color space, and reprojection (Ch27) because the final consumer — an eye's photoreceptor mosaic — has a roughly fixed, dense, uniform sampling grid. A phosphene-based interface has a wildly different "output device": a few hundred discrete elements, non-uniformly and unpredictably mapped to visual-field locations (§8), that must be driven by a scene-simplification model (§11) rather than a framebuffer blit. The relevant analog in this book is closer to the GPU inference pipelines in Ch229 and Ch238 than to any display presentation path in Ch20 or Ch27 — the "last mile" becomes a machine-learning model choosing what to convey, not a hardware scanout engine copying a buffer.
- **There is no swapchain in the conventional sense.** OpenXR's frame loop (Ch27) exists to hand timed, correctly-reprojected frames to a display with a fixed refresh rate. A neural interface's relevant timing constraints are physiological (how fast neurons can be safely and distinguishably re-stimulated) rather than a display panel's refresh rate, and its "resolution" is a channel count two to three orders of magnitude below even a low-end panel (§7) — closer to a very coarse, non-uniform sensor array being driven in reverse.
- **Passthrough and camera capture stay the same; everything downstream of it does not.** The camera-to-scene pipeline this book documents for existing AR headsets (Ch166, Ch250's MRUK) is exactly what a phosphene-based system would still need for its input side — nothing about capturing the world changes. What changes is everything after that: instead of compositing that camera feed into a rendered scene for a panel, the pipeline would need to run the kind of semantic-segmentation-driven scene-simplification inference described in §11, at real-time rates, on a channel budget nothing in this book's existing rendering pipelines targets.
- **"Frame time" and "latency" mean something closer to their meaning in robotics or haptics than in graphics.** The book's usual latency budget (Ch27's discussion of reprojection and motion-to-photon latency) is about not making a sighted user with a working visual system nauseous. A neural interface's relevant latency and safety constraints are about neural stimulation safety limits and closed-loop feedback, a different discipline (implantable neurostimulator engineering) that this book has not otherwise covered and does not attempt to cover in depth here.

None of this is a roadmap for this book to eventually document as a shipping stack — per §12 and §13, it may never arrive as a consumer display technology at all. It is included because the question "what would even change" is a useful way to see, by contrast, how much of the rest of this book's stack — DRM modesetting, Wayland surface composition, OpenXR swapchains, Meta's XR SDK layer — is built on the assumption that the endpoint is an eye with a fixed, dense, well-understood sampling geometry. Remove that assumption, and very little of the presentation half of this book's stack survives unchanged; the capture and scene-understanding half survives largely intact.
